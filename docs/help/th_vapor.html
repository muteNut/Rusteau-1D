Theory and equations

Coupled water, vapor and heat flow

With Vapor flow switched on, liquid water and water vapor move under gradients of the pressure head and of the temperature Philip and de Vries (1957); Saito et al. (2006). The water flow equation becomes

∂θT/∂t = ∂/∂x [ KLh (∂h/∂x + cos α) + KLT ∂T/∂x + Kvh ∂h/∂x + KvT ∂T/∂x ] − S
SymbolMeaning
θTtotal water content, θT = θL + θv, the liquid water content plus the water vapor content expressed as an equivalent water content
KLhisothermal hydraulic conductivity of the liquid phase [L/T] (the conductivity K(h) of the hydraulic model)
KLTthermal hydraulic conductivity of the liquid phase [L²/K/T] (temperature dependence of the surface tension)
Kvhisothermal vapor hydraulic conductivity [L/T]
KvTthermal vapor hydraulic conductivity [L²/K/T]

The vapor conductivities follow from the vapor diffusivity in the soil air, the saturated vapor density and the relative humidity hr = exp(h M g / (R T)) of the soil air (M molecular weight of water, g gravitational acceleration, R gas constant). The thermal vapor flux is multiplied by the enhancement factor η(θ) of Cass et al. (1984), which accounts for the higher thermal gradients in the air-filled pores and liquid islands (Further options: switchable).

The heat transport equation includes the transport of latent heat by the vapor flux and the convection of sensible heat by liquid water and vapor (heat transport); water flow and heat transport are solved sequentially in every time step. The surface boundary condition is usually the surface energy balance.

As in HYDRUS, the capacity term of the vapor content contains the unit conversion in a form that is exact for metres; with cm or mm the results differ slightly (e.g. 3.4 % in the cumulative surface flux of the HYDRUS example EnBal2b when changing from cm/d to m/h). Rusteau keeps the HYDRUS expression so that its results match HYDRUS 4.17.
