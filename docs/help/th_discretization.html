Theory and equations

Notes on spatial and temporal discretization

The accuracy of the numerical solution depends on the size of the finite elements and the time steps. Following the HYDRUS-1D help Šimůnek et al. (2009):

Water flow

  • Use small elements where large hydraulic gradients occur: near the soil surface (infiltration, evaporation), at the interfaces of materials with very different properties, and around the water table. Elements of 0.1–1 cm near the surface of a soil with infiltration or evaporation are typical van Dam and Feddes (2000).
  • Elements may grow with depth where the gradients are small; neighbouring elements should not differ in size by more than a factor of about 1.5. Use fixed points with relative element sizes on the page Soil profile.
  • Infiltration into very dry, fine-textured soil forms a sharp wetting front that needs fine elements and small time steps. Soils with n close to 1 benefit from the air-entry value of −2 cm.
  • Check the mass balance: relative errors above 1 % call for a finer mesh or smaller time steps.

Solute and heat transport

Two dimensionless numbers characterize the discretization of the advection–dispersion equation:

Pe = q Δx / (θ D) (grid Peclet number)
Cr = q Δt / (θ R Δx) (Courant number)

with the flux q, the element size Δx, the dispersion coefficient D, the retardation factor R and the time step Δt. Numerical oscillations are small when Pe ≤ 5 and Cr ≤ 1; oscillations can be virtually removed with Pe·Cr ≤ 2 Perrochet and Berod (1993). Rusteau, as HYDRUS, limits the time step so that the criterion Pe·Cr ≤ Pecrit is met, and adds artificial dispersion or uses upstream weighting where it is not (solute transport: general). Both reduce oscillations at the cost of numerical dispersion: the front is smeared more than with the real dispersivity.

Practical rules:

  • choose Δx at most about twice the dispersivity (Pe ≤ 2);
  • the maximum Peclet and Courant numbers of a run are shown on the page Run-time information;
  • with very small dispersivities (sharp fronts) refine the mesh rather than relying on artificial dispersion.

Moving fronts

Wetting fronts and solute fronts move through the profile; a mesh that is fine only near the surface may be too coarse once the front reaches deeper layers. Uniform fine meshes are safe for short profiles; for deep profiles refine where the front is expected during the period of interest.
